## Stylometric reading

The prose voice is investigative-analyst, not academic. Citations arrive in parenthetical clusters: `(MHLW filing 2022-R-0447; TIOJ March 2023; JPO Patent JP-2021-123456)`. There are no footnotes, no bibliography section, and no DOI resolution. The author trusts the reader to either recognize the reference system or to tolerate opacity. That choice signals confidence born of repetition: this is someone who has read enough MHLW filings that document numbers feel more natural than URLs.

The domain vocabulary is precise without being impenetrable. "Heat-not-burn" is distinguished from "vaporized nicotine" with regulatory consequences noted. patent claims are summarized rather than quoted, suggesting the author reads Japanese IP filings habitually enough to paraphrase rather than paste. The overall register is dry, compressed, and faintly exhausted: the tone of someone who has done this fifteen times and sees no reason to perform enthusiasm.

What is absent is as telling as what is present. There is no executive summary, no "key findings" callout, and no conclusion urging policy action. The dossier does not argue; it accumulates. That passivity is a stylistic fingerprint. Argument is for clients. Accumulation is for personal archives.

## Absence of citation infrastructure

For all its sourcing discipline, the dossier contains zero citation infrastructure. There is no linkchecker. There is no dead-link watchdog. There is no version snapshot of the MHLW filing pages, which are government websites and rot on schedule. There is no ArchiveFS packaging of the primary sources, despite the estate shipping a `ARFS\x01\x02` chunking format precisely for smuggling large payloads through git remotes. See `/setup/hardware-and-runtime` and `/archaeology/sunk-cost-blueprint` for the container format's rationale.

A TIOJ statistic cited in 2023 is a string of digits in a markdown file. When the ministry restructures its reporting tables, that citation becomes unverifiable. The dossier ages the moment it lands. The author either knows this and does not care, because the artifact is personal rather than operational, or knows this and has not yet wired the swarm's archiving behavior back into manual workflows. Either reading is unflattering. A 15,000-word document built on government filings deserves a companion archive. The absence is a gap in the estate's hygiene, and it mirrors the broader problem documented in `/archaeology/state-and-dependency-lifecycle`: manual processes persist where automation is theoretically available but practically unloved.
